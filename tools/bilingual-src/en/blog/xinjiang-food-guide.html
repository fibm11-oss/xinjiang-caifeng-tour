Food

What to Eat in Xinjiang: From Samsa to Big-Plate Chicken

Five categories, what to order and what to skip — no more guessing at the menu.

1. First, the logic of Xinjiang food

Xinjiang is a paradise of meat and fruit. Its cooking grew out of the Silk Road and nomad life: enormous portions of wheat and lamb, sweet lamb with no gamey taste, and fruit that is almost illegally sweet. But first-timers are easily put off by "cut cake" hustles in the bazaars or over-touristed kebab stalls. Three things to remember: wheat and lamb rule; portions are huge — one plate of noodles or one naan is a meal, don't estimate by inland standards; and the cuisines of the Uyghur, Kazakh, Hui and Han peoples overlap, so the same dish tastes different city to city.

2. Staples: naan, laghman, pilaf, samsa

Must-try: Samsa (thin-skinned baked bun) — thin pastry, big filling, best straight out of the oven; Naan is the "national bread", with sesame, oily and nest varieties, and a big naan keeps for days as trail food; Laghman (hand-pulled noodles) served with stir-fried toppings, and you can usually refill the noodles free; Pilaf (polo) is rice, lamb, carrot and onion steamed in one pot, and the wok-fried batch at lunch is the one to get; Ding-ding fried noodles cut the dough into cubes, even more flavourful.

  • Portions are huge — order less first, add more later
  • Pilaf tastes best at lunch; the evening batch is often microwaved

3. Grilled meats: red-willow, lamb skewers, pot meat, fish

Must-try: Red-willow skewers thread lamb on young willow twigs, the wood aroma seeping into the meat, with more depth than iron skewers; charcoal lamb skewers, fat and lean alternating with cumin and chilli, are best from a street stall grilling to order; pot meat stews lamb, potato and tomato in an enamel mug, one per person; Burqin grilled dogfish from the Irtysh is fine-flaked and boneless, perfect at the riverside night market.

  • Beware meat that is unnaturally bright red or over-marinated — it may hide colouring or tenderiser; find a stall that cuts and threads to order
  • Skip "seafood BBQ" in landlocked cities — freshness is a gamble

4. Soup noodles: ququ, soup rice, fen soup

Must-try: Ququ is the Xinjiang wonton — thin skin, fresh filling, sweet lamb broth, good for breakfast or late night; sour-soup dumplings and soup rice drop noodle pieces into a tomato broth, warming and appetising; fen soup is a thick lamb-and-vermicelli soup saved for festivals.

  • The breakfast hu-la soup is spicy and salty — go easy on day one

5. Dairy and sweets: yogurt, milk skin, maraqi

Must-try: Handmade yogurt is thick and tart, drinkable only with sugar or honey, and a chilled bowl at the night market cuts the grease; milk skin is the cream film on top of boiled milk, rich but not greasy, great with naan; maraqi is Xinjiang's handmade ice cream, milky and dense; san is a festival fried pastry, crisp and fragrant.

  • "Cut cake" (maren tang) is often sold by weight on the spot, with opaque pricing — buy at a proper shop or ask first
  • If you're lactose intolerant, go easy on the dairy

6. Fruit: eat by the season

Must-try: Turpan grapes (Aug–Sep), seedless white and mare's-teat varieties, very sweet; Hami melon (Jul–Sep), a netted muskmelon with strong aroma; Korla pears (Sep–Oct), thin-skinned and juicy, said to shatter when dropped; Aksu apples, watermelon and melon through autumn; and Southern Xinjiang apricots, dried apricots and all kinds of dried fruit — sweet, for snacking or as gifts.

  • Fruit is extremely sweet — watch it if you're diabetic; check stall hygiene on pre-cut fruit

7. Ordering rules of thumb

  • Order less, add more — refill the noodles rather than leave a full table
  • Ask the boss "what's freshest today" — more reliable than the menu
  • With big-plate chicken, add a portion of belt noodles to soak up the sauce — that's where the magic is
  • Tell vegetarians in advance; Xinjiang is meat-first, and last-minute veg options are thin
In one line: Xinjiang's goodness is in "just-grilled, just-cooked, just-slaughtered" freshness — the more local and the simpler the shop, the safer. Skip the influencer spots; join the queue the locals are in.
